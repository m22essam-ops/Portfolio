# The prompt for writing the pre-filled emails

Paste everything between the lines into ChatGPT, Claude, or whatever you use.
It already contains your own writing as the style reference, so you should not
have to explain yourself.

When you get versions back you like, put them into the editor, section 6, one
per box.

---

## THE PROMPT

You are writing copy for Mohammed Essam, a senior creative copywriter. Cairo,
then Dubai, now Madrid. His portfolio is mohammedessam.com and its whole
concept is "Award-losing copywriter (so far)".

### The job

On his site, clicking his email address opens the visitor's mail app with a
message **already written in it**. Not written by him. Written **as if by the
person emailing him**: a recruiter, a creative director, a founder.

So the visitor opens their mail app and finds a draft of their own email to
him, already there, better than the one they were going to write.

I need **three versions**. One is picked at random per visit.

### The joke, and it is the whole thing

It must read like **a template nobody bothered to fill in**. Square brackets
are left in, on purpose, exactly like the recruiter emails he actually gets:

    Hi [FIRST NAME], we are hiring a [ROLE] at [AGENCY].

The gag lands three ways at once:
1. He is doing the sender's job for them, which is a flex.
2. It admits what these emails always are: a form letter.
3. The blanks are funnier than the words. Put the joke **in the bracket**
   where you can, not around it.

The reader should smile, fill in three blanks, and hit send.

**The bracket is where the writing happens.** A bracket that is only a blank
is wasted. `[CITY]` is a blank. `[CITY WITH TERRIBLE WEATHER]` is a joke. Aim
for the second one every time.

### WHO THE JOKE IS ABOUT, which is the only rule that matters

The sender has to be willing to send this from their work email with their
boss copied in. That does NOT mean the brackets have to be polite. It means
one specific thing:

**A bracket may joke about the SENDER: their own agency, their own city,
their own budget, their own deadline, their own product.** Self-deprecation
is charming, it is what people already do in real emails, and it makes the
sender look self-aware rather than stupid. Keep all of it.

    [AGENCY THAT PAYS ON TIME]              good, it is a boast
    [CITY WITH TERRIBLE WEATHER]            good, everyone slags off their own weather
    [A NUMBER THAT IS LOWER THAN YOU EXPECT] good, disarming, and recruiters do say this
    [UNREASONABLE NUMBER] hours             good, the deadline is nobody's fault
    [VAGUE PROMISE OF FUTURE WORK]          good, honest about their own offer
    [DELIVERABLE NO ONE ASKED FOR]          good, their product, their joke

**A bracket may NOT put a judgement on a NAMED THIRD PARTY, and may NOT
insult the person receiving it.** Those are the two that make an email
undeliverable, because a real client name goes into that slot, in writing,
from a work account.

    [PRODUCT NOBODY NEEDS]      no, a named client's product
    [BRAND THAT WILL GHOST US]  no, a named client, and it is a screenshot
    [CLIENT WITH NO MONEY]      no, same
    [SOMEONE WHO USES CHATGPT]  no, it insults him, and it is his own joke
                                turned back on him
    "the strategist gave up"    no, a colleague who might be copied in

The fix for a failing bracket is almost never to remove the joke. Move it one
step away from the person: a category instead of a client
(`[THE KIND OF BRAND YOU WOULD EXPECT]`), a thing instead of a colleague
(`the strategy has been rewritten [TOO MANY] times`), the sender instead of
the recipient (`I will keep writing it myself, badly`).

### Hard rules

- **Subject and body.** Plain text. Line breaks are fine, no formatting.
- **Under 900 characters** for subject and body together. It becomes a URL.
- **No em dashes.** He hates them. Commas, full stops, colons.
- **Keep the square brackets.** At least three per version, in CAPITALS.
- **Never mention the website, the joke, or that the email was pre-written.**
  The moment it winks at itself it dies. It is a straight email that happens
  to be funny.
- It has to be **actually sendable**. A real person has to be willing to press
  send on it without feeling stupid. Funny, but not a sketch.
- **Written to him, not about him.** Second person.

### His voice, in his own words

These are all real lines from his site. Match this, do not describe it.

**The undercut. He sets something up and takes it away in the same breath:**

> If I play, I play to win. / I WON NOTHING
> Odds of winning: historically High. Ticket has no cash value and neither
> did the awards.
> More than 5 years of writing for people who skip the ads.
> Award-losing copywriter so far.

**The turn is always the last clause. Never the middle:**

> I fought the AI and the AI won, so I moved to easier fights now: managing
> my MacBook's storage to open one more Adobe file.
> A cheese pun with the cheese legally removed.
> Have your cake and live in it.

**Short. Often one sentence. Sometimes three words:**

> Fine, go. I'll wait.
> It rhymed.
> Never left the deck.
> Nothing here yet. Give it a minute.
> Broke, but rich in the head.

**He ends on a dare, not a plea:**

> Someone has to win eventually, feel lucky today?
> ...think you can give it a shot?
> You really opened the console on a copywriter's site? yes, it's vibe coded,
> happy now?

**Concrete and domestic, never abstract.** MacBook storage. Adobe files. His
room. Noise-cancelling headphones. Not "passion", not "impact", not "journey".

**Casual punctuation is allowed** when the voice is loose: lowercase i,
ellipses, a comma splice. It should read typed, not drafted.

### What kills it

Anything earnest. Anything motivational. Anything that sounds like an agency
wrote it. "Passionate", "storytelling", "impact", "journey", "synergy",
"I'd love to pick your brain". Exclamation marks. Emoji. Rhetorical questions
that are not dares. Anything that explains the joke.

### Failed attempts, so you do not repeat them

These were rejected. Learn from why.

> **Opportunity at [AGENCY NAME]**
> Dear [FIRST NAME], I came across your profile and thought of you
> immediately for a [JUNIOR / MID / SENIOR] copywriter role at [AGENCY NAME]
> in [CITY].

**Why it failed:** it is just a real recruiter email with brackets added. The
brackets are doing all the work and none of them are funny. No undercut
anywhere. It is an observation about templates, not a joke.

> **Not a recruiter, emailing anyway**
> I am [YOUR NAME] and I do [WHAT YOU DO]. Nobody sent me. If that is enough
> to go on, reply. If it is not, delete this and I will assume the worst.

**Why it failed:** the voice is close but it is *his* voice in the sender's
mouth, and it reads as him talking to himself. The sender would never write
"I will assume the worst" about themselves. Funny, wrong speaker.

> **A [ROLE] job at [AGENCY]**
> The job is [WHAT IT ACTUALLY IS, IN ONE LINE], it starts [WHEN], and the
> money is [A NUMBER]. Free for a call on [A DAY]?

**Why it failed, and this is the one to watch:** every bracket is safe, every
bracket is dull. This was written by over-correcting a real problem, and the
correction ate all the comedy: it swapped `[CITY WITH TERRIBLE WEATHER]` for
`[WHERE]` and `[AGENCY THAT PAYS ON TIME]` for `[AGENCY]`. Sendable and
pointless. **If the brackets are only blanks, you have written a form, not a
joke.** The rule above exists to save the jokes, not to remove them.

### Write

Give me **three versions**, numbered, each as:

    SUBJECT: ...
    BODY:
    ...

Make the three genuinely different in attack. Not three drafts of one idea.
One might be brisk and corporate with the blanks doing the damage, one might
be a person being honest, one might be barely an email at all.

Before each, give me **one line** on what the version is doing, so I can tell
them apart quickly.
